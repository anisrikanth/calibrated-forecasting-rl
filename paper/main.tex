\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with a proper scoring rule is increasingly used to train language models to forecast, but it is rarely clear what such training changes: how the model uses its evidence, or only the level and spread of its probabilities. We study this in a small, fully controlled setting. We train Llama~3.2~3B Instruct with GRPO and a Brier-score reward on 1,391 resolved Kalshi questions about scheduled U.S.\ economic releases. Each prompt contains only the series' recent results, all public before the question was asked. We evaluate on 410 later questions against simple rules built from the same evidence, with intervals from resampling whole releases. Three findings stand out. First, the untrained model cannot use the evidence: its AUC is 0.52, while counting how many recent results would have resolved YES reaches 0.85. A deterministic block of ``key numbers'' that restates the comparison raises its AUC to 0.76 with no training. Second, RL improves the model only when the evidence is legible and GRPO's division of advantages by the group's standard deviation is turned off. Over three seeds, the Brier score improves by 0.031 (95\% interval 0.013 to 0.049) and AUC by 0.070 (0.029 to 0.115), with calibration unchanged. That brings the model level with a logistic regression on the same features, but behind gradient-boosted trees. Third, with the default scaling, all three seeds collapse within about 130 steps onto a 0/1 copy of a single cue: has the latest result already crossed the threshold? Under the training reward itself, this rule scores worse than a calibrated version of the same rule. Without legible evidence, or with shuffled outcomes, RL instead hedges toward a constant. \todo{One sentence on the pre-registered test-set result.} All experiments ran on free Kaggle T4 GPUs, about 30 GPU-hours in total.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Several groups now train language models to forecast with reinforcement learning, rewarding them with a proper scoring rule on questions whose outcomes are known \citep{turtel2025outcome,turtel2026proper,chandak2025scaling,afifi2026research,ye2026alphabet,han2026futureworld}. The headline metric is usually the Brier score, and it usually improves. But the Brier score mixes three things: whether the average forecast matches the base rate, how spread out the forecasts are, and whether the model puts higher probabilities on the questions that resolve YES \citep{murphy1973partition}. A model can lower its Brier score a long way by moving every forecast toward the base rate, which improves mean calibration and nothing else \citep{vancalster2016hierarchy}. Most of these systems also retrieve news or search the web, so a gain can come from better searching, better reading or better forecasting. Only one of these papers reports discrimination for the trained model. There, a Brier reward improved the Brier score from 0.186 to 0.165 but AUC only from 0.725 to 0.751 \citep{turtel2026proper}.

We ask a narrower question in a setting built to answer it: \emph{with the evidence held fixed, does proper-score RL teach a small model to use that evidence?} Kalshi's markets on scheduled economic releases suit this well. Every question has a numeric threshold (``Will CPI rise by more than 0.3\% in May?''), and every series has a history of comparable past results. That history lets us build the evidence from public data with settlement timestamps, so nothing the model sees postdates the question. It also lets us write simple rules over the same evidence as baselines, and the market price at question time gives a strong external benchmark that the model never sees.

We train Llama~3.2~3B Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} with GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} and a Brier reward on 1,391 questions from 2024 and 2025, and evaluate on 410 questions from 2026. Ten training runs cross two prompts with two settings of GRPO's advantage scaling, repeat the key conditions over three seeds, and add a shuffled-outcome control. Our findings:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The untrained model's failure is reading its evidence.} With six recent results in the prompt, it scores worse than always saying 0.5 (AUC 0.52), while a one-line counting rule over the same numbers reaches AUC 0.85. Adding a deterministic ``key numbers'' block raises the untrained model's AUC to 0.76. An ablation traces the gain to three lines: the YES condition restated, the latest result compared with the threshold, and a count of recent results that would have resolved YES (\S\ref{sec:elicitation}).
\item \textbf{RL helps only when the evidence is legible and GRPO's std scaling is off.} Of the four prompt-by-scaling conditions, only this one improves on its untrained model in both Brier score and AUC: $-0.031$ and $+0.070$ averaged over three seeds, with calibration unchanged. With outcomes shuffled, the same setup gets worse. So the gain comes from the outcomes, not from the training process (\S\ref{sec:twobytwo}).
\item \textbf{With GRPO's default scaling, RL collapses onto a lookup rule.} In all three seeds, the share of near-certain answers passes 90\% within about 140 steps. More than 98\% of evaluation answers end up exactly 0 or 1, matching a one-cue rule: ``1 if the latest result already satisfies the question, else 0.'' Under the training reward itself, this rule scores worse than the same rule stated at its hit rates (0.847 vs 0.870 average reward). This extends \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated}'s analysis of std-scaled GRPO from synthetic and biology tasks with a log-score reward to real forecasting with a Brier reward (\S\ref{sec:collapse}).
\item \textbf{Without usable evidence or a real signal, RL hedges.} With the plain prompt, or with shuffled outcomes, unbiased RL squeezes the forecasts toward a single number. That is the proper-score optimum for a forecaster whose answers carry no information (\S\ref{sec:twobytwo}).
\item \textbf{RL brings the model up to a logistic regression, not past simple models.} The best RL runs tie a logistic regression on five key-number features and trail gradient-boosted trees on discrimination (AUC $-0.03$ to $-0.04$, intervals excluding 0). The market is far ahead of everything (Brier 0.062; \S\ref{sec:simple}). Nothing transfers to held-out weather questions (\S\ref{sec:weather}).
\end{enumerate}

Methodologically, we report AUC and a shift-matched baseline next to every Brier score, and compute intervals by resampling whole releases. We compare every model with simple rules built from its own prompt. Our final test-set evaluation follows comparisons fixed in advance and committed to the repository before it ran. Code, prompts, per-question answers and the reward-hacking log are public.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/anisrikanth/calibrated-forecasting-rl}}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{RL for language-model forecasting.} \citet{turtel2025outcome} train a 14B reasoning model with GRPO-family algorithms and a Brier-type reward on Polymarket questions with retrieved news. They report that plain GRPO made 39\% of forecasts extreme. \citet{turtel2026proper} compare five proper scoring rules on a 120B model without std scaling, and are the only group to report AUC and a bias--information--noise decomposition \citep{satopaa2021bin}. \citet{chandak2025scaling}, \citet{afifi2026research}, \citet{ye2026alphabet} and \citet{han2026futureworld} train models from 3B to over 100B parameters with search or retrieved news. \citet{levy2025rl} trains 1.5B to 7B models against market prices and outcomes. Two hedging failures resemble ours. A Brier-only reward led \citet{chandak2025scaling}'s model to answer ``Unknown'' about 40\% of the time, and training on outcomes collapsed \citet{levy2025rl}'s model to always saying 50\%. Our setting differs in holding the evidence fixed and small (six numbers), so that what RL changes can be measured against rules over the same numbers.

\paragraph{Forecasting benchmarks and markets.} LLM forecasters approach but do not reach human crowds and markets \citep{halawi2024approaching,karger2025forecastbench,zou2022autocast}. Live and market-based benchmarks are now common \citep{zeng2026futurex,yang2025prophetarena,nel2025kalshibench}, and small untrained models often lose to the base rate \citep{li2026canllms}. Kalshi's macro markets are as accurate as consensus forecasts and futures, or more so \citep{diercks2026kalshi}. Low-priced Kalshi contracts are overpriced \citep{burgi2026makers}, which matters mostly for our weather brackets.

\paragraph{Training stated confidence.} RL with a proper scoring rule makes models' stated confidence in their own answers much better calibrated, and in one case also much more discriminative (AUROC 0.59 to 0.86) \citep{lin2022teaching,tian2023just,leng2025taming,baniharouni2026rewarding,damani2026beyond}. There the signal, whether the model knows the answer, is already inside the model. In our setting it has to be read from the prompt, and the contrast is part of what we test.

\paragraph{What GRPO changes.} GRPO scores each answer against the other answers to the same question \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. Dividing by the group's standard deviation biases training \citep{liu2025understanding}. With a log-score reward on stochastic yes/no outcomes it drives forecasts to the extremes, while PPO, RLOO and GRPO without the division do not \citep{bereket2025uncalibrated}. RL for reasoning mostly sharpens what the base model already samples \citep{yue2025doesrl}, and even random rewards can change behaviour through GRPO's clipping \citep{shao2026spurious}. That motivates our shuffled-outcome control.

\paragraph{Language models and numbers.} LLMs can extrapolate numeric sequences \citep{gruver2023llmtime} but make basic comparison errors \citep{yang2025numbercookbook}. Removing the LLM from LLM-based time-series forecasters often does not hurt \citep{tan2024useful}, so we report simple rules and a logistic regression in every table.

\paragraph{Evaluating forecasters.} Questions that resolve together share drivers, so effective sample sizes are far below question counts \citep{paleka2025pitfalls}. We therefore resample whole releases. We use the Brier score \citep{brier1950verification}, its Murphy decomposition \citep{murphy1973partition}, proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007strictly}, and the ladder consistency checks of \citet{paleka2025consistency}.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data}

We pull every resolved market from eight Kalshi economic series, January 2024 to October 2026. The series are month-over-month and year-over-year CPI, both headline and core; nonfarm payrolls; the unemployment rate; the Fed funds rate after each meeting; and the Fed's rate decision (cut, hold or hike, by size). Each release is an \emph{event} with a ladder of thresholds (``more than 0.1\%'', ``more than 0.2\%'', \dots), giving 2,296 questions in 249 releases. Each question is asked 24 hours before its market closes. Its evidence is the six most recent results of the same series whose markets \emph{settled} strictly before the question time, read from Kalshi's settlement values. Settlement follows the public release, so this is conservative; an audit finds no context entry dated at or after its question.

We split by release date. The oldest 70\% of releases are for training (177 releases, 1,391 questions, January 2024 to December 2025, yes-rate 0.491). The next 15\% are for validation (36 releases, 410 questions, January to May 2026, yes-rate 0.461), and the newest 15\% for testing (36 releases, 495 questions, June to October 2026, yes-rate 0.396). Every threshold of a release stays in one split. All development decisions used the validation split; the test split is scored once (\S\ref{sec:test}). As an out-of-domain set we use daily high-temperature brackets for New York and Chicago. They are never trained on; we score 60 days sampled from 2026 (360 questions; yes-rate 0.167).

Llama~3.2 was released in September 2024 with a stated knowledge cutoff of December 2023. All evaluation questions are from 2026, so pretraining cannot have seen their outcomes. Some 2024 training outcomes predate the model's release; that affects what the model trains on, not how it is evaluated. As a benchmark we take the market price at question time, from the last hourly candle before it (the bid--ask midpoint if the spread is at most 0.20, otherwise the last trade). The model never sees it.

\subsection{Prompts}

The system prompt asks for brief reasoning and a final line \texttt{Probability: 0.XX} (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). The user message gives the date, the market's resolution rule as the question, and the recent results, oldest first. We call this the \textbf{plain} prompt. The \textbf{key-numbers} prompt adds a deterministic block computed from the same inputs, with five lines:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item the YES condition in plain words;
\item the latest result and its distance from the threshold;
\item the change from the result before;
\item how many recent results would have resolved YES;
\item the recent average and range.
\end{enumerate}
The block adds no information the prompt did not already contain; it does the comparison a 3B model gets wrong. Fed-decision questions have no numeric threshold and get no block.

\subsection{Training}

We train LoRA adapters \citep{hu2022lora} (rank 32, $\alpha=32$, all attention and MLP projections; 48.6M trainable parameters) with TRL's GRPO trainer \citep{vonwerra2020trl} and Unsloth \citep{unsloth}, sampling answers with vLLM \citep{kwon2023vllm}. Each question gets 4 sampled answers, and each step uses 4 questions. One pass over the training questions is 347 steps. We use learning rate $10^{-5}$ with cosine decay and 10\% warm-up, no KL penalty, and TRL's default token-level (DAPO) loss \citep{yu2025dapo}; full settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}. The reward for an answer stating probability $p$ on a question with outcome $y \in \{0,1\}$ is
\[
r = \underbrace{1 - (p-y)^2}_{\text{Brier reward}} + \underbrace{0.5 \text{ if a readable probability line, else } -1}_{\text{format reward}},
\]
and an unreadable answer gets Brier reward 0. GRPO's advantage for answer $i$ in a group of $G=4$ is
\[
A_i^{\text{std on}} = \frac{r_i - \bar r}{\operatorname{std}(r) + \epsilon} \qquad\text{or}\qquad A_i^{\text{std off}} = r_i - \bar r .
\]
The first is TRL's default (\texttt{scale\_rewards="group"}); the second removes the division, as in Dr.\ GRPO \citep{liu2025understanding}. Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists the runs.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Each model answers each question 4 times (temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.95, at most 320 tokens, fixed seed), and its forecast is the average; an unreadable answer counts as 0.5. We report the Brier score, log loss (probabilities clipped to $[0.01, 0.99]$), calibration error (ECE, 10 equal-width bins) and AUC. AUC is the chance that a random YES question gets a higher forecast than a random NO question; it ignores the level of the forecasts. Every comparison is paired on the same questions. Its 95\% interval comes from 4,000 bootstrap draws that resample whole releases, since thresholds of one release resolve together \citep{paleka2025pitfalls}. For groups of runs (three seeds), each draw averages the group's runs on the same resampled releases. The interval then covers question sampling, not seed variation, and we report the spread across seeds separately.

\paragraph{Baselines on the same evidence.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Base rate:} each series' training yes-rate.
\item \emph{Counting rule:} the share of the six recent results that would have resolved YES.
\item \emph{Latest-result rule:} 1 if the latest result already would have, else 0.
\item \emph{Logistic regression:} five features from the key numbers, fitted on training questions with an L2 penalty.
\item \emph{Gradient-boosted trees:} the same five features, with and without the series as a categorical feature, settings chosen by cross-validation on the training questions with each release kept in one fold.
\end{itemize}
Questions without a threshold get the base rate. We also report a \emph{shift-matched} untrained model, moved up or down to the trained model's average forecast, so that a gain from moving the average alone is visible.

\subsection{The untrained model cannot read its evidence}
\label{sec:elicitation}

With the plain prompt, the untrained 3B model scores Brier 0.272 and AUC 0.520 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). That is worse than always saying 0.5, and worse than its own score with the recent results removed (0.253). The evidence is not the problem: the counting rule over the same six numbers scores 0.168 and AUC 0.846. The model reads the numbers but does not compare them with the threshold. Its reasoning and its final probability often disagree, and about half of adjacent thresholds in a ladder are ordered the wrong way.

The key-numbers block raises the untrained model's AUC from 0.520 to 0.757 (+0.237, interval +0.18 to +0.30) and lowers its Brier score to 0.201, the first model setting to beat the base rate. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} reveals the block one line at a time, still with no training.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Restating the YES condition alone} raises AUC by 0.093 (+0.040 to +0.146). Part of the failure is parsing Kalshi's legal-style rule text.
\item \textbf{Adding the latest result against the threshold} raises it a further 0.06.
\item \textbf{Adding the count of recent results that would have resolved YES} raises it a further 0.12. These three lines give the best untrained prompt: Brier 0.190, AUC 0.789, ECE 0.055.
\item \textbf{The change and range lines add nothing.} The full block is within noise of the three-line version.
\item \textbf{The count needs the latest result next to it.} The YES condition plus the count alone scores clearly worse than the full block (AUC $-0.048$, $-0.094$ to $-0.002$).
\item \textbf{Asking the model to estimate the number first does not help.} With the plain context, ``estimate the value, then compare it with the threshold'' scores the same as the plain prompt (AUC 0.522).
\end{itemize}
So the bottleneck is reading and comparing the numbers, not the order of the reasoning.

\paragraph{Without the key numbers, one pass of RL moves the level, not the ordering.} Run 1 lowered every forecast by about 0.06 on both evaluation sets. On held-out weather, where one bracket in six resolves YES, that alone improved the Brier score significantly (0.286 to 0.254). But the untrained model shifted down by the same amount scores better still (0.242), and AUC did not improve. With twice the training at twice the learning rate (run 2), AUC did rise (0.52 to 0.69, +0.10 to +0.25), but 72\% of forecasts became near-certain and the Brier score got worse (0.320). With std scaling off (run 7), RL squeezed every forecast into 0.36 to 0.71. That is the proper-score optimum for a forecaster whose answers do not depend on the evidence: the training yes-rate for every question.

\paragraph{With the key numbers and std scaling off, RL improves the forecasts.} Averaged over three seeds, the Brier score falls by 0.031, log loss by 0.072 ($-0.118$ to $-0.024$) and AUC rises by 0.070, with calibration error unchanged. The seeds barely differ (Brier 0.169 to 0.171, AUC 0.820 to 0.832), and each seed's interval alone excludes zero for Brier and AUC. All three seeds improve on core CPI (both measures), year-over-year CPI, the Fed funds rate and unemployment. None improves on payrolls, the noisiest series (Appendix~\ref{app:series}).

\paragraph{The gain comes from the outcomes.} Run 8 repeats run 4 but reassigns outcomes at random across the training questions, keeping the yes-rate. Evaluated on real outcomes, it is \emph{worse} than the untrained model (Brier $+0.035$, $+0.007$ to $+0.059$) and much worse than run 4 (Brier $+0.067$, AUC $-0.120$). Its forecasts squeeze toward one number, as run 7's do: spread 0.074, 39\% of forecasts between 0.45 and 0.55. Its answers keep their reasoning (median 90 tokens) and are all readable, so the difference from runs 4 to 6 is the forecasts, not their format or length.

With GRPO's default scaling, all three key-numbers seeds turn into the same rule (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). More than 98\% of their answers are exactly 0.00 or 1.00, and almost all of those are the latest-result rule's answer. Averaged over the seeds, they cannot be told apart from the rule (Brier $+0.001$, $-0.017$ to $+0.017$; AUC $+0.007$, $-0.006$ to $+0.023$). Against the untrained model with the same prompt, the Brier score does not improve ($+0.017$, $-0.038$ to $+0.080$), while log loss ($+0.396$) and calibration error ($+0.156$, $+0.038$ to $+0.204$) get much worse. The std-off seeds beat the std-on seeds on every metric:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Brier $-0.048$ ($-0.099$ to $-0.003$);
\item log loss $-0.468$ ($-0.773$ to $-0.198$);
\item calibration error $-0.148$ ($-0.199$ to $-0.019$);
\item AUC $+0.045$ ($+0.018$ to $+0.077$).
\end{itemize}

The collapse happens on the same schedule in every seed: a third of the way through the pass (Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}). Run 10 departs from the rule on 25 threshold questions, 10 of them payrolls. On most of these its four 0/1 answers disagree with each other, so its averaged forecast lands between 0 and 1. Some of those answers follow the count of recent results instead of the latest one. Shrinking answers is not part of the collapse: run 3's answers fell to a median of 7 tokens, but runs 9 and 10 kept short reasoning and still answer 0 or 1.

\paragraph{The collapse is not what the reward asks for.} On the training questions, the latest-result rule is right 84\% of the time when it says YES and wrong 15\% of the time when it says NO. Stated at those hit rates (0.84 and 0.15), it earns a higher average Brier reward (0.870) than its 0/1 version (0.847). A Brier reward therefore prefers the calibrated rule, but std-scaled GRPO drove all three seeds to the 0/1 version. Dividing each answer's advantage by its group's reward spread inflates small differences between answers that already agree. With a proper scoring rule, that favours confident answers and feeds on itself \citep{bereket2025uncalibrated}.

\paragraph{During training, both settings learn to rank.} Training-time AUC rises from about 0.72 to 0.80--0.85 with std scaling on or off (Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}). What differs is the probabilities: with scaling on, near-certain answers climb from about 20\% to 98\% between steps 75 and 140 and calibration error rises; with it off, answers stay graded. On held-out questions the std-off runs keep their AUC gain (0.82 to 0.83), while the std-on runs sit at the rule's 0.77 to 0.80. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows the end states. Std-on runs end with two spikes at 0 and 1, std-off runs keep a spread, and the shuffled and plain std-off runs pile up around 0.55.

\subsection{Comparison with simple models}
\label{sec:simple}

The best RL runs reach the logistic regression on the same five features. The difference is within noise: Brier $+0.008$ ($-0.035$ to $+0.041$), AUC $-0.028$ ($-0.061$ to $+0.006$). They trail gradient-boosted trees on discrimination:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item against trees with the series feature, AUC $-0.042$ ($-0.071$ to $-0.015$), Brier $+0.019$ ($-0.007$ to $+0.045$);
\item against trees without it, AUC $-0.033$ ($-0.057$ to $-0.010$).
\end{itemize}
In Brier skill relative to the base rate, RL takes the untrained model from 0.17 to about 0.30; the logistic regression reaches 0.33, the trees 0.38 and the market 0.75 (Appendix~\ref{app:skill}). The market prices information that is not in our prompt, such as consensus forecasts and nowcasts. Our claim is about what RL does with fixed evidence, not about beating the market.

\subsection{No transfer to weather}
\label{sec:weather}

No method has any discrimination on the held-out temperature brackets except the market. Every rule built from recent highs has AUC below 0.5, and the base rate (Brier 0.139) beats every model and rule. RL does not change this. The std-off runs score 0.208 to 0.223, against 0.223 for the untrained model with the same prompt. The std-on runs are worse (0.261 to 0.298; three-seed average $+0.060$, $+0.038$ to $+0.080$), because they carry their 0/1 answers over.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What RL learned.} With legible evidence and unbiased advantages, RL taught the model to weigh its cues. Its forecasts became graded and better ordered, its calibration held, and the gain depended on real outcomes. What RL did not do is find information beyond the features: the model ends level with a logistic regression and behind gradient-boosted trees on the same evidence. A proper scoring rule makes a forecaster calibrated relative to the information it actually uses. When the model's answers do not depend on the evidence, either because it cannot read it (plain prompt) or because the reward carries no signal (shuffled outcomes), the optimum is a constant, and that is what RL found. In our runs, one pass of RL improved the use of evidence the model could already read. Only with twice the training at twice the learning rate (run 2) did it learn some ordering from the plain prompt, and then at the cost of calibration.

\paragraph{Practical lessons.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Turn off GRPO's std scaling with proper-score rewards.} Our three-seed comparison shows the default produces a collapse that a Brier score alone would not reveal: the collapsed runs' training Brier score improved. This matches \citet{bereket2025uncalibrated}, and the recent forecasting papers that drop the scaling \citep{turtel2026proper,ye2026alphabet}.
\item \textbf{Compare trained models with rules built from their own prompts.} The collapse was obvious only next to the latest-result rule, and the honest ceiling only next to the trees.
\item \textbf{Report AUC and a shift-matched baseline next to the Brier score.} Run 1's significant weather gain was a uniform shift.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Scope.} We study one model size and family, LoRA training for one pass at one learning rate, and one domain: scheduled numeric releases.
\item \textbf{Sample size.} Validation has 36 releases, so per-series results are descriptive. Settings were chosen on that one split, which is why the final numbers come from a test split scored once.
\item \textbf{The key-numbers block is hand-designed for threshold questions.} The general lesson is that evidence must be legible to the model, not that this block is right elsewhere.
\item \textbf{Training-time curves are not clean forward tests.} Questions from the same release can be rewarded before others are answered.
\item \textbf{Context order.} During the late-2025 U.S.\ government shutdown, delayed releases put one pair of months out of calendar order in the context of 50 validation questions. Both values were public; prompts were left unchanged so every run sees identical inputs.
\item \textbf{The market is a benchmark, not ground truth.} Low-priced contracts are biased \citep{burgi2026makers}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Holding the evidence fixed shows when proper-score RL helps a small language model forecast. When the model cannot read its evidence, RL moves the level of its forecasts or hedges them toward a constant. When the evidence is made legible, RL with GRPO's default std scaling turns the model into a 0/1 lookup rule, while RL without that scaling improves the forecasts and brings the model up to a logistic regression on the same features. We hope the controls used here become standard in RL-for-forecasting work: rules built from the prompt, shuffled outcomes, shift-matched baselines, seeds, and AUC next to the Brier score.

\section{Prompt}
\label{app:prompt}

\paragraph{System prompt.}
\begin{quote}\small
You are a careful forecaster. You will get a yes/no question about an upcoming data release or measurement, the current date, and sometimes recent results for the same data series. Think it through briefly (under 150 words), then end with one final line in exactly this form: Probability: 0.XX where 0.XX is your probability, between 0 and 1, that the question resolves YES.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{User message (a training question, key-numbers prompt).} The plain prompt is the same without the key-numbers block. The question's date is also passed to Llama's chat template.
\begin{quote}\footnotesize\ttfamily\raggedright
Current date: Tuesday, June 11, 2024, 8:25 AM Eastern\\[3pt]
Question: If the Consumer Price Index (CPI) increases by more than 0.1\% in May 2024, then the market resolves to Yes.\\[3pt]
Recent results in this series (oldest first; all were public before this question):\\
- CPI in Nov 2023: 0.1\\
- CPI in Dec 2023: 0.3\\
- CPI in Jan 2024: 0.3\\
- CPI in Feb 2024: 0.4\\
- CPI in Mar 2024: 0.4\\
- CPI in Apr 2024: 0.3\\[3pt]
Key numbers (worked out from the question and the recent results above):\\
- This question resolves YES if the result is above 0.1\%.\\
- Latest result (CPI in Apr 2024): 0.3\%, 0.2 percentage points above the threshold.\\
- Change from the result before: -0.1 percentage points (from 0.4\% to 0.3\%).\\
- Recent results that would have resolved YES: 5 of 6.\\
- Recent results: average 0.3\%, lowest 0.1\%, highest 0.4\%.\\[3pt]
What is the probability that this question resolves YES?
\end{quote}
This question resolved NO (May 2024 CPI was flat); the market priced YES at 0.28.

\section{Reward hacks and parser issues}

We logged every issue that changed what the reward measured; \texttt{writeup/hacks.md} in the repository has the full log.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]\raggedright
\item \textbf{Parser.} The answer ``\texttt{Probability:~8/17~=~0.47}'' was read as 8 and rejected. We now parse the last number on the last probability line.
\item \textbf{A uniform shift that looks like a held-out gain.} Run 1's weather improvement; caught with AUC and a shift-matched baseline.
\item \textbf{Overconfidence with more training.} Run 2 under std scaling.
\item \textbf{Training-time metrics overstate learning.} The yes-minus-no gap grows with sharpening alone. We judge runs on held-out AUC.
\item \textbf{Collapse onto the latest-result rule.} Runs 3, 9 and 10; fixed by turning off std scaling.
\end{enumerate}
